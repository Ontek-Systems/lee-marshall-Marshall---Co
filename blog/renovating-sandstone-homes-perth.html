Renovating a Sandstone Home

An old Perthshire stone house is built to work in a way modern materials fight against. Here is what that means when you come to do it up.

20 August 2026  ·  8 min read  ·  Perth, Perthshire & Kinross

Most of the damp we get called out to in old Perth properties was not there when the house was built. It arrived later, put there by somebody trying to help.

There is a lot of stone in this part of the country. Terraces and villas through Perth, farmhouses and cottages out through Perthshire, steadings and estate properties around Dunkeld, Crieff and Blairgowrie. A great deal of it is well over a hundred years old and standing perfectly square, which tells you the people who built it knew what they were doing.

The trouble is that they built to a completely different set of rules from the ones a modern housebuilder works to, and if you renovate an old stone house as though it were a new build, you will cause damage. Not straight away. Usually five to fifteen years later, which is long enough for whoever did it to be well out of the picture.

The one idea that explains nearly everything

A modern house keeps water out. A traditional stone house lets water in and lets it back out again.

That sounds like a fault. It is not, it is the design. A solid stone wall has no cavity and no damp proof course. When it rains, and it rains plenty here, the outer face of that wall takes up moisture. Between showers, that moisture evaporates back out, mostly through the mortar joints, which were made of lime precisely because lime is softer and more permeable than the stone around it. The joints are the escape route. The wall breathes, gets damp, dries, and carries on doing that for two centuries without complaint.

The mortar is meant to be the weak part. That is the whole point of it.

Once you understand that, most of the classic mistakes explain themselves.

Mistake one: cement pointing

At some stage, and around here a lot of it happened between the sixties and the eighties, somebody decided that repointing an old wall in hard cement mortar was an improvement. It looks tidy when it is done. It is also the single most damaging thing commonly done to a Scottish stone building.

Cement is harder and far less permeable than lime, and usually harder than the sandstone itself. The wall still takes up water, because you have not stopped that. But now the moisture cannot leave through the joints. So it leaves through the next softest route available, which is the face of the stone. Over the years that pushes the surface of the stone off in flakes and layers, which is what people mean by spalling. You end up with crisp tidy joints standing proud of stone that is crumbling back between them.

And what does not evaporate outward has to go somewhere. Often it works inward instead, and shows up as damp patches on internal walls that no amount of injected treatment or fresh plaster will fix, because the problem is not rising damp. It is a wall that has been sealed shut.

The fix is unglamorous. Rake out the cement carefully without wrecking the arrises of the stone, and repoint in an appropriate lime mortar. It takes time, it takes patience, and it costs more than running a cement mix round with a gun. It is also the difference between a wall that stops deteriorating and one that keeps going.

Mistake two: sealing the outside

Same logic, different product. Cement render over stone, modern impermeable masonry paint, and the water repellent sealers that get sold as a cure for damp all do the same thing. They reduce how much water goes in, which sounds like a win, and they reduce how much can get back out, which is the part nobody mentions.

Water will always find a way in somewhere. Round a window, at a cracked cill, through a defective rone or a blocked gutter, up from a raised path or a flowerbed piled against the wall. Once it is in behind a sealed surface it is trapped, and it will sit in the wall doing damage through every frost we get.

If you have a rendered stone house and the render is cracked and drummy, hiding it under another impermeable coat is not a repair. Nine times out of ten the more useful money is spent on the boring things first: clearing and repairing the gutters and downpipes, sorting the cills and copes, and getting ground levels back down away from the base of the wall.

Before you spend on anything else

  • Check the rones and downpipes in heavy rain, not on a dry day. That is when you see what is actually happening.
  • Look at ground levels. Decking, patios, monoblock and raised borders built up against a stone wall cause an enormous amount of the damp we see.
  • Look up at the skews, copes and chimney heads. Water getting in at the top of a wall travels a long way down before it shows.
  • Only then start thinking about the wall itself.

Mistake three: insulating without thinking it through

This is the one that worries us most, because it is being done more now, not less, and it is usually done with good intentions.

A solid stone wall is cold and it loses heat, so people want to insulate it. Fair enough. But you cannot fit a cavity wall, so the insulation goes on the inside face. The moment you do that, you make the stone wall behind the insulation colder than it used to be, because you are no longer letting any warmth from the house get into it. Colder wall, less drying, and a surface inside the build up where warm moist air from the house can reach dewpoint and condense out of sight.

Done properly, with a build up designed to handle vapour and manage moisture, internal insulation on a traditional wall works. Done as a quick job with an impermeable board glued straight onto old plaster and a bit of tape round the edges, it can rot timbers you cannot see. Joist ends built into external walls are the usual casualty, and you find out about them years later when a floor starts to move.

Our position on this is simple. If you want to insulate a stone wall, that is a design decision, not a product decision. It needs thinking about as a whole build up including what happens at the joist ends, at the window reveals, and at the wall head. Anybody who quotes you for it off a photograph has not thought about it.

Sash and case windows

The other regular conversation. Original sash and case windows in a Perthshire property are usually made from slow grown timber that is better than anything you can buy off a shelf today, and in most cases they are repairable even when they look past it. Rot tends to be local, at the bottom rail and the cill, and a splice or a new section of timber is a genuine repair rather than a patch.

Two things worth knowing before you decide:

  • If the property is in a conservation area or is listed, replacing them with modern uPVC units may well not be permitted anyway. That decision may already be made for you.
  • Most of what people dislike about old windows is draught and noise, not the windows themselves. Proper draught proofing, overhauled cords and pulleys, and secondary glazing where it is appropriate will deal with the great bulk of that, and you keep the timber and the proportions that make the house look right.

Sometimes replacement genuinely is the answer, and we will tell you when we think it is. But a hundred year old window that needs a hundred pounds of joinery is not a window that needs skipped.

What good looks like on an old house

If you are getting quotes for work on a traditional property in Perth or anywhere across Perthshire, the questions worth asking are not really about price. They are about whether the person quoting understands what they are working on.

  1. Did they ask what has already been done to the building, or did they just measure up?
  2. Did they look at the gutters, the ground levels and the chimney heads before talking about damp treatment?
  3. Can they tell you why they are specifying lime rather than cement, in their own words?
  4. If insulation is proposed, can they explain what happens at the joist ends?
  5. Are they treating the symptom or the cause?

Old stone buildings are not difficult to work on. They are just different, and they are unforgiving of anybody who treats them as though they are not. Work with how the building was designed to behave and it will keep standing long after all of us. Fight it, and it will lose slowly, expensively, and out of sight.

The short version

  • A solid stone wall is meant to take up water and let it back out. Do not seal it shut.
  • Cement pointing and impermeable coatings cause spalled stone and internal damp. Lime lets the wall breathe.
  • Most damp in old Perth properties starts at a gutter, a cill, a chimney head or a raised ground level.
  • Internal insulation on a stone wall is a design job, not a product you buy.
  • Sash and case windows are usually repairable, and in a conservation area that may be the only option anyway.
