Do You Need a Building Warrant?

Planning permission and building warrants are two different things, and most folk in Perthshire only find that out halfway through a job. Here is the plain version.

20 August 2026  ·  9 min read  ·  Perth, Perthshire & Kinross

If you take one thing from this page, take this. In Scotland you need a building warrant before the work starts, not after it, and it is the homeowner who is responsible for having it. Not the builder. The homeowner.

We get asked about this most weeks. Somebody has read an English blog about building regulations, or a neighbour has told them their cousin in Stirling did a loft without any paperwork, and they arrive at the first meeting either terrified of the council or convinced none of it applies to them. Usually the truth sits somewhere in the middle, and it is a lot less painful than people expect once you know which box your job falls into.

So here it is, written the way we would explain it standing in your kitchen with a mug of tea.

Planning permission and a building warrant are not the same thing

This is where most of the confusion starts. They are two separate applications, they go to two separate teams at Perth and Kinross Council, and they are asking two completely different questions.

Planning permissionBuilding warrant
Should you be allowed to build this here? Is what you are building safe and up to standard?
Cares about size, siting, appearance, impact on neighbours, and the character of the area. Cares about structure, fire, insulation, ventilation, drainage, and safe access.
Not always needed. Plenty of work is permitted development. Needed for most structural work regardless of whether planning was required.
Neighbours may be notified. It can be objected to. No neighbour consultation. It is a technical check, not a popularity contest.

You can absolutely need one without the other. A loft conversion that sits entirely inside the existing roof might not need planning permission at all, but it will almost certainly need a warrant because you are altering structure, forming a stair, and changing how people escape a fire. Equally, you might get planning permission for something and still have to sort the warrant separately before anybody lifts a shovel.

What a building warrant actually is

Scotland does not use the English building regulations system. We work under the Building (Scotland) Act 2003 and the Building (Scotland) Regulations 2004. Under that system, a building warrant is written permission from the local authority to carry out the work, granted on the basis of drawings and specifications submitted in advance.

Three things about it that catch people out:

  1. It comes first. The warrant has to be granted before work begins. Carrying out warrantable work without one is an offence, and liability is not limited to the homeowner. It can extend to the builder and to whoever ordered the work.
  2. It lasts three years. A warrant is valid for three years from the day it is granted, with the possibility of an extension if the job runs on. So getting it early does not mean you have to start on Monday.
  3. It is not finished until the completion certificate is accepted. When the work is done, a completion certificate is submitted and the council either accepts it or comes back with points to sort. The job is not signed off until they accept it.

Why the completion certificate matters more than people think

It is the piece of paper your solicitor will be asked for when you sell the house. We have seen more than one sale in Perth held up because a previous owner built an extension years ago and never closed out the warrant. Sorting it retrospectively is possible but it is slower, dearer, and it happens at the worst possible moment, when you have already accepted an offer.

Whose job is it, yours or ours?

Legally it is yours. The warrant sits against your property, the application is made in your name or by an agent acting for you, and the completion certificate is submitted by you as the relevant person.

In practice, on our jobs, we handle it. We will get the drawings prepared, put the application in as your agent, deal with the queries that come back, and make sure the certificate is submitted properly at the end. You should not have to learn the building standards system to get a kitchen extension.

But be wary of any builder who waves the whole subject away. If somebody tells you that you do not need a warrant for a job that plainly needs one, they are not saving you money. They are transferring risk onto you and keeping it off themselves.

A builder who tells you the paperwork does not matter is telling you something about how the rest of the job will go.

Is it only for big jobs? No, but it is not for every job either

This is the bit worth reading twice, because a fair amount of ordinary domestic work needs no warrant at all. The Scottish building standards technical handbooks set out the work that does not require one. Here is roughly how it lands on the sorts of jobs we get asked about around Perth and Perthshire.

Usually needs a warrant

  • House extensions, single or double storey
  • Loft conversions, because you are altering structure and forming escape
  • Garage conversions into habitable space
  • Removing or altering a load bearing wall, or putting in a steel
  • New or altered drainage and waste connections
  • Installing a gas or oil fired boiler
  • Anything that increases the floor area of the house
  • Converting a building from one use to another, for example a steading into a dwelling

Often does not need one

  • Replacement windows and doors, including where the frame is being replaced
  • Open raised decking, provided it is under 1.2m in height and is not forming an escape route or accessibility access
  • A detached single storey outbuilding over 8 square metres but not exceeding 30 square metres, ancillary to the house, subject to conditions on its distance from the house and boundary, and provided it has no fixed combustion appliance or sanitary facility in it
  • Low voltage electrical work, at or below 50 volts AC or 120 volts DC
  • Fitting a new kitchen or bathroom on a like for like basis where you are not touching structure, drainage layout or floor area
  • Internal work such as replacing finishes, non structural partitions in some cases, and general redecoration

Read this before you rely on the list above

Those are the general positions and they all carry conditions in the actual regulations. Garden rooms are the classic example. Under 8 square metres is treated differently from the 8 to 30 band, and the minute you put a wood burner or a toilet in it, or move it too close to the boundary, the answer changes. Decking changes the moment it goes above 1.2m.

The full lists are in Section 0.5 of the Scottish Government domestic technical handbook, and the procedure is set out in the building standards procedural handbook. If your job is anywhere near a line, ask the building standards team at Perth and Kinross Council, or ask us and we will check it for you. Correct at the time of writing, August 2026.

Important: exempt from a warrant does not mean exempt from the standards

This one is subtle and it is worth understanding, because it is where people get properly caught out.

Work being listed as not requiring a warrant means you do not have to apply for permission first. It does not mean the work is unregulated, and it does not mean the building standards stop applying. A deck under 1.2m still has to be safe. Replacement windows still have to meet the requirements for ventilation, safety glazing and, where it applies, escape from the room. You just do not need to go and get a warrant before doing it.

So "no warrant needed" is never the same as "anything goes." It only means the paperwork route is different.

Planning permission around Perth and Perthshire

On the planning side, Scotland has its own permitted development rules for houses, and they are genuinely different from the English ones. Anything you read about the English larger home extension scheme and its 6m and 8m allowances does not apply here. Ignore it.

The things that most often decide the answer locally:

  • Is it a flat? Householder permitted development rights apply to houses. Flats are treated differently, which matters a great deal in central Perth where a lot of the stock is subdivided.
  • Is it in a conservation area? Permitted development is cut back significantly in conservation areas. Perth city centre has substantial coverage, and so do parts of Dunkeld, Crieff, Auchterarder, Blairgowrie and Kinross.
  • Is the building listed? In Scotland listings are Category A, B and C, not the English grades. If your property is listed you will likely need listed building consent as well as anything else, and that covers internal work too, which surprises people.
  • Which elevation is it on? Work to the front or principal elevation, and anything facing a road, is treated far more tightly than work at the rear.
  • How much have you already used? Previous extensions by previous owners count against your allowance. The house does not get a fresh start when it changes hands.

If you are out in the countryside there is another layer again. Septic tank discharges are regulated by SEPA rather than the council, and adding bedrooms can push an existing system past what it was designed to serve. Private water supplies, radon protection in the areas where it is required, and access for plant on a long unmade track all need thought early rather than late. We deal with that side of things regularly around Comrie, Bankfoot, Stanley, Methven and Alyth.

A realistic order of events

People always want to know how long all this takes. It varies with the council's workload and with how complete your drawings are when they go in, so we will not quote you a number we cannot stand behind. What we can give you is the order things happen in, because getting the order wrong is what actually costs people months.

  1. Site visit and an honest conversation about what is achievable and what it is likely to cost
  2. Check the planning position: house or flat, conservation area, listed, what has been built before
  3. Measured survey and drawings
  4. Planning application if one is needed
  5. Structural design and calculations where there are steels or altered structure
  6. Building warrant application, with the queries that come back dealt with promptly
  7. Warrant granted, and only then does work start on site
  8. Inspections at the agreed stages as the work progresses
  9. Completion certificate submitted and accepted, and the job is properly closed

The single biggest delay we see is an application going in half finished to save a fortnight, then sitting waiting on information that could have been supplied at the start. Get it right first time and the whole thing moves faster.

What if the work is already done?

It happens, usually because somebody bought a house with an extension that was never signed off, or a previous trade cut a corner. You are not stuck. A late application can be made, but two things are worth knowing. The fees are higher for a late application, and the standards applied are the ones in force at the time you apply, not the ones that were in force when the work was built. That second point can be expensive if the work is old.

Our advice is always to deal with it when you find it rather than when you are trying to sell.

The short version

  • Planning permission and a building warrant are separate. You may need one, both, or neither.
  • The warrant comes before the work, and it is the homeowner's legal responsibility, though we handle it for you.
  • Extensions, lofts, garage conversions, structural alterations, drainage and boilers generally need a warrant.
  • Replacement windows, decking under 1.2m, small outbuildings within the size limits and like for like internal work generally do not.
  • No warrant needed still does not mean the standards do not apply.
  • The completion certificate is what closes the job. Do not leave it hanging.
